When navigating oral pain or complex dental issues, patients are often surprised to learn how drastically training differs between general dentists and dental specialists.

Every licensed dentist begins with four years of dental school. But when a dentist chooses a specialty, they commit to thousands of additional hours in accredited post-doctoral residency programs. Understanding these differences helps clarify who is best equipped to evaluate specific oral symptoms, from bite forces and root recession to nerve pathways.

Below is a breakdown of clinical training hours across the dental profession, followed by an in-depth look at what orthodontists do beyond straightening teeth with braces.

---

## Specialty Training Hours Compared

![Dental Specialist Clinical Training Hours](/dental-specialty-training-hours.svg)

### 1. General Dentist (DDS or DMD)
- **Dental School:** 4 years (approximately 4,800 total educational and clinical hours).
- **Mandatory Residency:** None required in most jurisdictions. General dentists are licensed to practice all phases of dentistry immediately upon graduation.
- **Optional Residencies:** Some complete a 1-year Advanced Education in General Dentistry (AEGD) or General Practice Residency (GPR), adding approximately 2,000 clinical hours.
- **Primary Scope:** Comprehensive oral examinations, restorative fillings, crowns, routine extractions, and preventative care.

### 2. Orofacial Pain Specialist
- **Residency Length:** 2 years full-time (accredited by CODA).
- **Additional Residency Hours:** Approximately 4,000 hours.
- **Total Estimated Training:** ~8,800 hours.
- **Primary Scope:** Non-odontogenic facial pain, Burning Mouth Syndrome (BMS), trigeminal neuropathies, temporomandibular disorders (TMD), and sleep-related breathing disorders. Residents rotate through neurology, headache centers, and anesthesiology clinics.

### 3. Orthodontist (Orthodontics and Dentofacial Orthopedics)
- **Residency Length:** 3 years full-time (36 months).
- **Additional Residency Hours:** Approximately 6,000 hours.
- **Total Estimated Training:** ~10,800 hours.
- **Primary Scope:** Biomechanics of tooth movement, craniofacial growth modification, occlusal harmony, and skeletal realignment.

### 4. Periodontist (Periodontology)
- **Residency Length:** 3 years full-time surgical residency.
- **Additional Residency Hours:** Approximately 7,500 hours (50 to 60 hours per week).
- **Total Estimated Training:** ~12,300 hours.
- **Primary Scope:** Hard and soft tissue supporting structures, bone grafting, periodontal ligament mechanics, gum recession, surgical implant placement, and occlusal trauma on supporting bone.

### 5. Prosthodontist (Prosthodontics)
- **Residency Length:** 3 years full-time.
- **Additional Residency Hours:** Approximately 8,500 hours (demanding intensive laboratory fabrication and complex clinical rehabilitation).
- **Total Estimated Training:** ~13,300 hours.
- **Primary Scope:** Complex full-mouth reconstructions, occlusal vertical dimension restoration, maxillofacial prosthetics, and precision implant restorations.

### 6. Oral and Maxillofacial Surgeon (OMFS)
- **Residency Length:** 4 to 6 years hospital-based residency (frequently paired with a medical doctor degree).
- **Additional Residency Hours:** 16,000+ hours (often exceeding 80 hours per week on hospital surgical call).
- **Total Estimated Training:** ~20,800+ hours.
- **Primary Scope:** Orthognathic jaw reconstruction, complex surgical extractions, facial trauma surgery, pathology resection, and bone reconstruction.

## What Orthodontists Do Beyond Braces

Most people associate orthodontists exclusively with aesthetic braces or clear aligners. However, orthodontics is formally defined as **Orthodontics and Dentofacial Orthopedics**. Their specialty training focuses heavily on bone biology, biomechanics, and skeletal alignment.

Here is what orthodontists manage beyond conventional tooth straightening:

### 1. Dentofacial Growth Modification
In growing children and adolescents, orthodontists do not simply move teeth; they guide the growth of the facial skeleton:
- **Palatal Expanders:** Widen a constricted upper jaw bone to improve nasal airway volume and prevent crossbites.
- **Functional Appliances:** Devices such as the Herbst appliance or Twin Block posture the lower jaw forward to encourage mandibular remodeling in patients with severe overbites.
- **Headgear and Reverse Pull Facemasks:** Apply targeted orthopedic force to redirect upper jaw growth.

### 2. Airway and Sleep Apnea Management
The position of the upper and lower jaws directly dictates the volume of the upper airway:
- Orthodontists screen for pediatric airway obstruction, mouth-breathing, and enlarged adenoids.
- By expanding narrow dental arches, orthodontists increase space for the tongue, preventing it from collapsing backward into the airway during sleep.
- In adult patients with mild to moderate obstructive sleep apnea, orthodontists fabricate custom Mandibular Advancement Devices (MADs) to maintain an open airway overnight.

### 3. Temporomandibular Joint and Bite Force Balancing
When teeth do not meet evenly, jaw muscles and the temporomandibular joint (TMJ) must compensate:
- **Canine Guidance:** Ensuring that during side-to-side jaw movements, the canines carry the force and immediately disclude (separate) the back teeth, protecting molars and jaw muscles from overload.
- **Bite Force Distribution:** Eliminating premature tooth collisions (occlusal interferences) that can trigger chronic muscle spasms, clenching, and periodontal ligament inflammation.

### 4. Surgical Orthodontics (Orthognathic Coordination)
When skeletal discrepancies are too severe to fix with tooth movement alone, orthodontists work side by side with oral and maxillofacial surgeons:
- The orthodontist decompensates and aligns the teeth within each separate jaw arch over 12 to 18 months.
- The oral surgeon performs orthognathic surgery (such as a Le Fort osteotomy or bilateral sagittal split osteotomy) to physically move the upper or lower jaw into correct skeletal alignment.
- The orthodontist then fine-tunes the bite once bone healing is complete.

### 5. Interdisciplinary Pre-Prosthetic Alignment
Before a general dentist or prosthodontist can place implants, bridges, or veneers, adjacent teeth may be tipped or drifted into the empty space:
- Orthodontists upright tipped molars, parallel dental roots, and reopen collapsed spaces so implants can be placed with ideal axial loading.
- They extrude broken teeth to bring sound tooth structure above the gumline, avoiding surgical crown lengthening.

### 6. Cleft Lip and Palate Craniofacial Care
Orthodontists are central members of hospital craniofacial teams:
- In infants with cleft palate, they use Nasoalveolar Molding (NAM) appliances to reshape the nasal cartilage and alveolar ridge before primary surgical repair.
- Throughout childhood, they manage arch alignment, expand the palate before bone grafting, and coordinate dental development into adulthood.

### 7. Oral Habits and Myofunctional Correction
Chronic habits alter bone formation and muscle memory:
- Orthodontists design appliances (such as tongue cribs) to intercept persistent tongue thrusting or thumb sucking.
- They collaborate with myofunctional therapists to retrain tongue resting posture against the roof of the mouth, supporting nasal breathing and long-term oral stability.

## Summary

General dentists provide the foundational day-to-day care of teeth and gums. Specialists like periodontists and orthodontists spend 6,000 to 7,500 additional hours mastering surgical tissue biology and biomechanical force distribution.

When oral symptoms like localized burning or bite pressure persist, understanding these distinct training backgrounds helps you connect with the right clinician to inspect the underlying hardware.
