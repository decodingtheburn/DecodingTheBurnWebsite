If you run your fingernail along your gumline and feel a sharp, notch-shaped wedge in the side of your premolar or canine, you are looking at an abfraction&nbsp;lesion.

When you show that notch to your dentist, there is an 80% chance they will look at it and say:
> *"You are brushing too hard with a hard-bristled toothbrush. Switch to an ultra-soft brush and brush more&nbsp;gently."*

That explanation sounds plausible until you look at the basic physics of the oral cavity. Toothbrush bristles are soft nylon filaments. They cannot carve deep, angular, sharp-edged V-grooves into the dense enamel of a single tooth while leaving the adjacent teeth completely untouched.

The true culprit is not your toothbrush. It is **nocturnal clenching, lateral grinding, and biomechanical tooth&nbsp;flexure**.

When you understand how grinding creates abfraction notches, you uncover one of the most overlooked physical hardware triggers fueling Burning Mouth&nbsp;Syndrome.

---

## 1. The Physics of Abfraction: How Teeth Bend Under Lateral&nbsp;Torque

Dentists are trained to view teeth as rigid, inanimate blocks of porcelain and enamel. Biomechanically, teeth are nothing like rigid bricks: they are dynamic, viscoelastic living pillars anchored into alveolar bone by periodontal ligaments that function like miniature hydraulic shock&nbsp;absorbers.

When you bite straight down during normal chewing, compressive forces travel cleanly along the vertical long axis of the tooth root. Alveolar bone absorbs that load with zero structural&nbsp;damage.

Everything changes when you clench or grind your teeth laterally at&nbsp;night:
* **The Lateral Lever Arm**: During nocturnal bruxism, your jaw exerts horizontal shear torques exceeding 200 to 300 pounds of pressure per square inch. Because the opposing cusps slide across each other at an angle, the tooth functions as a lever&nbsp;arm.
* **The Cervical Fulcrum**: The crown bends laterally away from the root. The mechanical bending fulcrum concentrates directly at the cementoenamel junction (CEJ), right at the margin where the crown meets the&nbsp;gumline.
* **Tensile Failure at the Thinnest Point**: Enamel is hardest at the biting cusps (up to 2.5mm thick), but tapers down to a razor-thin 0.5mm edge at the gumline. While enamel handles compression exceptionally well, it has almost zero tensile flexibility. When the tooth flexes sideways, the microscopic hydroxyapatite crystals at the cervical collar are pulled apart under intense tensile stress. Over time, millions of micro-flexures snap the brittle enamel rods completely away, leaving a sharp, wedge-shaped notch called an **abfraction&nbsp;lesion**.

## 2. The Microscopic Gateway: Exposing 30,000 to 50,000 Tubules Per Square&nbsp;Millimeter

The real danger of an abfraction lesion is not cosmetic. The danger is what sits directly underneath the fractured enamel&nbsp;shield: **dentin**.

Dentin is not a solid, inert wall. Under a scanning electron microscope, dentin resembles a dense honeycomb packed with **30,000 to 50,000 microscopic conical channels per square millimeter**, known as dentinal&nbsp;tubules.

Each microscopic tubule contains:
1. **Dentinal Fluid**: A protein-rich hydrostatic fluid flowing outward under positive pulpal&nbsp;pressure.
2. **Odontoblastic Cell Processes**: Living cellular extensions reaching outward from the tooth's central pulp&nbsp;chamber.
3. **Unmyelinated Nerve Terminals**: Sensory A-beta, A-delta, and C-fiber nerve endings intimately wrapped around the tubule entryways, leading directly into the maxillary (V2) and mandibular (V3) branches of the trigeminal&nbsp;nerve.

According to Brännström's **hydrodynamic theory of dentin sensitivity**, any physical, thermal, or osmotic disturbance at the mouth of an exposed tubule causes rapid fluid movement inside the channel. That microscopic fluid movement physically deforms the mechanoreceptors and nociceptors at the pulp junction, firing instantaneous electrical impulses straight into the central nervous&nbsp;system.

## 3. The BMS Amplification Loop: Why Abfraction Becomes Round-the-Clock&nbsp;Fire

In a person with a normal, unprimed nervous system, an exposed dentinal tubule causes classic "dentin hypersensitivity." You drink a glass of ice water or eat a piece of chocolate, feel a sharp 2-second zinger in the tooth, and the sensation&nbsp;vanishes.

In a patient suffering from Burning Mouth Syndrome, the neurological landscape is completely different:
* **Peripheral and Central Sensitization**: The trigeminal pain pathways are already primed in a hyper-reactive state. Microscopic peripheral nerve fibers have sprouted excessive receptors, and central inhibitory gating in the brainstem has broken&nbsp;down.
* **Acid-Sensing Ion Channels (ASIC3 &amp; TRPV1)**: Unmyelinated C-fibers embedded around exposed tubules express dense clusters of Acid-Sensing Ion Channels. When you consume acidic coffee (pH ~5.0), citrus fruit (pH ~3.2), salad dressings (pH ~2.8), or carbonated water, the acid penetrates straight into the open tubules, locking the ASIC3 channels into an open firing&nbsp;state.
* **Bacterial Acid Reservoirs**: The sharp notch created by an abfraction lesion sits right at the gingival sulcus, where anaerobic biofilm accumulates. Bacteria ferment microscopic food particles into lactic and acetic acids, bathing the bare tubules in a continuous, 24-hour acidic&nbsp;wash.

Instead of registering a localized tooth twinge that lasts two seconds, the sensitized trigeminal system amplifies the signal into a continuous, radiating neuropathic scalding sensation that spreads across the tongue, lips, and mucosal&nbsp;tissues.

## 4. Why Class V Composite Patches Routinely Fail (The Engineering&nbsp;Mistake)

When patients finally convince a dentist to address their sensitive cervical notches, the standard clinical response is almost always the same: etch the tooth with acid and bond a Class V composite resin&nbsp;filling.

Yet countless BMS sufferers report that receiving cervical composite patches either failed to resolve their burning or made their symptoms significantly worse. There are three clear mechanical reasons&nbsp;why:

### 1. Bonding Over an Active Flexure Point
If a dentist fills an abfraction notch without evaluating and adjusting the lateral bite forces that caused the tooth to bend in the first place, the tooth continues to flex every single night during bruxism. Composite resin is rigid and brittle. Under repetitive bending stress, the composite bond breaks at the gingival margin, creating microscopic marginal gaps that pull in bacterial plaque and oral acids like a microscopic suction&nbsp;pump.

### 2. Sclerotic Dentin Resists Resin Adhesion
Longstanding abfraction notches develop **sclerotic dentin** as the pulp attempts to protect itself by calcifying the tubule lumens with mineral deposits. Sclerotic dentin is notoriously resistant to standard dental acid etching. Bonding resin over sclerotic dentin frequently results in weak hybrid layers and immediate micro-leakage.

### 3. Overcontoured Marginal Ledges
Because cervical notches sit at or below the delicate gumline, shaping and polishing composite resin in that sector is exceptionally difficult. Clinicians frequently leave an overcontoured restorative ledge. That synthetic shelf overhangs the gingival tissue, trapping anaerobic bacteria and generating chronic marginal inflammation right beside sensitized nerve&nbsp;trunks.

## 5. The Systems-Engineering Protocol for Abfraction and&nbsp;BMS

If you suspect that nocturnal grinding and cervical abfraction notches are feeding your burning mouth pain, you cannot solve the problem by slapping on random fillings or brushing with sensitive&nbsp;toothpaste.

You have to address the mechanical and biochemical sequence in the correct&nbsp;order:

### Step 1: Starve the Chemical Fuel
Before touching a tooth with a drill, stop pouring acid down open tubules. Shifting to an ultra-low acid baseline (neutral pH diet, eliminating citrus, vinegars, sodas, and fermentable carbohydrates) prevents dietary acids from stimulating open ASIC3 receptors and gives inflamed nerve endings immediate breathing&nbsp;room.

### Step 2: Perform a Forensic Occlusal Audit
Ask your dentist to perform an occlusal evaluation using articulating paper to identify **eccentric balancing interferences**. If your premolars or canines make premature, heavy contact during lateral excursions, those teeth are absorbing violent bending torques every time you swallow or clench at&nbsp;night.

### Step 3: Neutralize Nocturnal Bending Torques
Protect the teeth against lateral flexure using a custom-fabricated, hard acrylic occlusal guard adjusted with proper anterior guidance. A properly balanced appliance disengages the posterior premolars during lateral movement, eliminating the cervical bending torque that snaps enamel rods away at the&nbsp;gumline.

### Step 4: Seal Tubules with Biocompatible Materials
If cervical notches require restoration, work with a clinician who understands tooth flexure. Utilizing flexible resin-modified glass ionomer (RMGI), bioactive desensitizing sealants, or micro-hybrid composites placed only after occlusal interference has been eliminated ensures the dentinal tubules stay sealed without creating chronic marginal&nbsp;overhangs.

## The Bottom Line

Burning Mouth Syndrome is rarely a phantom, imaginary disease. It is a biological alarm system responding to physical breakdown across the oral&nbsp;hardware.

When nocturnal grinding flexes your teeth, snaps off the cervical enamel shield, and exposes tens of thousands of microscopic dentinal tubules to oral acids, the trigeminal nerve is simply doing its job: sounding the alarm on a mechanical breach that conventional medicine failed to&nbsp;see.
