When clinicians discuss dental demineralization, conversations almost always center on dental caries (cavities caused by Streptococcus mutans producing lactic acid). But for patients suffering from non-carious tooth sensitivity, cervical margin pain, and Burning Mouth Syndrome (BMS), the real culprit is ambient biocorrosion: the chemical dissolution of dental hard tissues by dietary acids, reflux, and acidic fluid pockets.

Understanding the precise biophysical numbers behind critical pH reveals why teeth can look completely free of cavities while microscopic nerve conduits are actively leaking fluid and firing neuropathic pain signals into the trigeminal system.

---

## 1. The Critical pH Thresholds: Enamel vs. Cementum vs. Dentin

The term **critical pH** represents the exact thermodynamic tipping point where oral fluids switch from being supersaturated with calcium and phosphate to being undersaturated. Above the critical pH, saliva remineralizes hydroxyapatite. Below this threshold, mineral ions physically dissolve out of the tooth crystal matrix into the oral cavity.

The critical pH is not a single number for the entire tooth. Different dental layers possess completely distinct mineral compositions, making exposed root surfaces dramatically more vulnerable than coronal enamel:

```
+------------------+-------------------+-----------------+-----------------------+
| Tissue Layer     | Mineral Content   | Critical pH     | Dissolution Velocity  |
+------------------+-------------------+-----------------+-----------------------+
| Enamel           | ~96% Mineral      | pH 5.5          | Baseline              |
| Dentin           | ~70% Mineral      | pH 6.0 - 6.2    | 2x to 4x faster       |
| Root Cementum    | ~45% - 50% Mineral| pH 6.2 - 6.7    | 7x to 10x faster      |
+------------------+-------------------+-----------------+-----------------------+
```

### Why Cementum Dissolves Near Neutral pH (6.2 to 6.7)
* **Carbonate Impurities:** Enamel consists of dense, tightly packed hydroxyapatite crystals. In contrast, root cementum and radicular dentin contain higher concentrations of carbonate and magnesium substitutions. These chemical impurities create structural strain in the crystal lattice, making cementum soluble at remarkably mild acidity.
* **Microscopic Thickness:** The acellular extrinsic fiber cementum (AEFC) covering the cervical root margin is only **20 to 50 micrometers thick** (thinner than a human hair). 
* **The Clinical Danger:** While it takes a strongly acidic liquid like lemonade or cola (pH 2.5 to 3.2) to strip healthy enamel, a mildly acidic coffee (pH 5.8 to 6.2), sparkling water (pH 5.5), or commercial salad dressing (pH 4.0) dissolves root cementum rapidly. Once this 30-micrometer protective shield dissolves, tens of thousands of fluid-filled dentinal tubules are exposed directly to the mouth.

## 2. Salivary Clearance and the Stephan Curve: How Long Does Acid Linger?

A common misconception among patients is that acidic food or drink only touches the teeth for the few seconds it takes to swallow. In oral physiology, this timeline is governed by the **Stephan Curve**, first documented by Dr. Robert Stephan in 1944.

When an acid enters the mouth, local pH drops almost instantaneously into the danger zone (pH 2.5 to 4.5). The critical question is: how long does it take for saliva to restore neutral pH (7.0 to 7.4)?

```
Oral pH
  7.4 ---------------------------------- Baseline Resting Saliva
  7.0
  6.5 ---------------------------------- Cementum Dissolves (pH 6.2 - 6.7)
  6.0
  5.5 ---------------------------------- Enamel Dissolves (pH 5.5)
  5.0
  4.0          \_______/
  3.0    Acid   Depression Phase (20–40 Minutes Clearance)
      Ingress
       0 min    10 min    20 min    30 min    40 min    50 min
```

### Key Factors Governing Acid Clearance Time:
1. **Unstimulated Saliva Flow:** Normal resting salivary flow is approximately 0.3 to 0.4 mL/minute. In a healthy mouth, bicarbonate buffering requires **20 to 40 minutes** to neutralize an acid challenge back to pH 7.0.
2. **Hyposalivation and Medication Use:** In patients with salivary hypofunction (induced by dehydration, mouth breathing, or common medications like antihistamines, blood pressure pills, or antidepressants), acid clearance can stretch to **60 to 90 minutes or longer**.
3. **Continuous Sipping:** Sipping an acidic beverage (such as iced coffee, kombucha, or lemon water) over the course of two hours keeps oral pH below the critical threshold of 6.2 continuously for hours at a time, completely preventing remineralization.

## 3. The Gingival Sulcus: A Hidden Trap for Acidity and Ionic Cross-Talk

While most dental textbooks measure pH in pooled saliva collected under the tongue, the micro-environment at the gumline tells a completely different story.

The **gingival sulcus** (the shallow microscopic crevice between the tooth root and the free gingival margin, normally 1 to 3 mm deep) behaves like a micro-fluidic dead zone:

* **Fluid Dynamics:** Inside the sulcus flows Gingival Crevicular Fluid (GCF). Unlike active salivary flushing in the open oral cavity, sulcular fluid has an extremely low flow rate (approximately 3 to 20 microliters per hour).
* **The Entrapment Phenomenon:** When dietary acids, sugars, or acidic oral products enter the gingival crevice, capillary action pulls them into the sulcular pocket. Because saliva cannot mechanically flush this tight collar, acidic hydrogen ions and bacterial fermentation products remain trapped against exposed cervical cementum and subgingival crown margins.
* **Sulcular pH Variations:**
  * **Healthy Sulcus:** In a quiescent, non-inflamed gingival margin, the resting GCF maintains a neutral to slightly alkaline pH of approximately **7.40 to 7.80**.
  * **Acid Ingress & Biofilm Fermentation:** When fermentable carbohydrates or low-pH liquids penetrate the sulcus, bacterial metabolism by anaerobic subgingival microflora rapidly drops local sulcular pH down to **5.80 to 6.20**.
  * **ASIC Receptor Firing:** Unmyelinated C-fibers and pulpal sensory nerves terminate directly along the dentin-cementum junction at the base of the sulcus. When the local sulcular pH drops below 6.8, Acid-Sensing Ion Channels (**ASIC1** and **ASIC3**) open, triggering inward sodium currents and firing persistent neurogenic signals into the trigeminal nucleus.

## 4. How to Accurately Check Oral and Salivary pH

Patients seeking to measure oral acidity often encounter conflicting results due to improper testing methodology. Here is how oral pH is clinically and objectively evaluated:

### 1. Narrow-Range pH Indicator Strips (Diagnostic Grade)
* Standard wide-range litmus paper (pH 1 to 14) is inadequate because it cannot resolve the small 0.2 to 0.5 pH shifts that dictate cementum dissolution.
* Use diagnostic test strips calibrated to a narrow range of **pH 5.5 to 8.0** with 0.2 or 0.3 increments.
* **Testing Protocol:**
  * Do not eat, drink, or brush teeth for at least 60 minutes prior to testing.
  * Swallow once to clear excess pooled saliva.
  * Allow fresh saliva to collect on the floor of the mouth for 30 seconds.
  * Touch the indicator strip directly to the saliva pool (or spit a small sample into a clean plastic spoon).
  * Read the color change immediately against the calibrated scale.
  * **Healthy Resting Target:** Saliva should measure between **pH 7.0 and 7.4**. A resting score of 6.5 or below indicates chronic salivary acidosis and depleted bicarbonate buffer capacity.

### 2. Digital Micro-Electrode pH Meters
* In specialized clinical research, micro-combination glass electrodes (or flat-surface antimony electrodes) allow clinicians to measure the exact pH of the dental pellicle, interproximal plaque, and the gingival margin directly without disturbing the fluid boundary layer.

### 3. Evaluating Buffer Capacity (Bicarbonate Challenge)
* Pure resting pH only tells half the story. High-caries and chronic burning mouth patients often suffer from diminished **buffering capacity**.
* Standardized in-office kits (such as the Saliva-Check BUFFER test) introduce an acid titrant to a saliva sample. If the saliva's bicarbonate concentration is robust, it rapidly resists the pH drop. If depleted, the pH plummets and stays depressed.

## 5. Practical Summary: The Biophysical Reality

* **Enamel dissolves at pH 5.5**, but **exposed root cementum and radicular dentin begin dissolving at pH 6.2 to 6.7**.
* A single sip of acid can keep tooth root surfaces demineralizing for **20 to 40 minutes** before saliva can restore balance.
* The **gingival sulcus** acts as a fluid trap where trapped acidity can trigger Acid-Sensing Ion Channels (ASIC3) and provoke trigeminal nerve cross-talk long before any structural hole or cavity is visible on a routine dental X-ray.
* If you have exposed roots, receding gums, or leaking restorations, protecting the cervical margins with targeted desensitizers, biocompatible glass ionomers, or high-pH mineralizing protocols is an essential physical defense against chronic oral pain.
