Alongside the burning sensations, there is another symptom that frequently baffles both patients and clinicians: sudden, phantom alterations in how your mouth&nbsp;tastes.

In medical literature, distorted taste is known as **dysgeusia**, and in the BMS community, it is one of the most common yet least understood complaints.

In my own case, I have had a constant salty taste directly behind my two top front teeth since April. It is localized, persistent, and never seems to go away regardless of what I eat or drink. 

Hearing from other patients reveals that these phantom taste profiles are widespread, ranging from salty and metallic to sour and chemical&nbsp;sensations.

## The Salty Taste Behind the Front Teeth

Having a localized salty taste concentrated right behind the upper central incisors is remarkably revealing from an anatomical standpoint.

Directly behind your two front teeth sits the **incisive papilla**, which covers the **incisive foramen**. This small bony channel is the exit point for the **Nasopalatine nerve** (a sensory branch of the Trigeminal nerve, V2) and contains a rich concentration of minor palatal salivary glands.

In my observation and literature review, a persistent salty sensation in this exact spot seems to point to two primary biological mechanisms:

### 1. Salivary Sodium and Bicarbonate Imbalances
When minor salivary glands become inflamed or hypofunctional due to local irritation, the composition of saliva shifts. Instead of a balanced, hypotonic fluid, saliva can become hypertonic, carrying a higher concentration of sodium and chloride ions that taste distinctly salty.

### 2. Neuropathic Spontaneous Firing
Taste perception is heavily intertwined with the Trigeminal nerve. When sensory nerve endings at the incisive papilla become irritated (whether from dental restorations, clenching, or localized acid exposure), the damaged fibers can fire spontaneous electrical impulses. The brain frequently misinterprets these stray neuropathic signals as basic taste sensations, most notably salt or metal.

## Why Standard Exams Miss the Explanation

When patients describe these weird tastes, dentists inspect the teeth and gums, find no visible infections, and conclude that everything is normal.

The challenge is that phantom tastes are rarely caused by visible surface sores. They stem from microscopic alterations in salivary electrolyte concentration, subgingival bacterial biofilm, or low-grade neuropathy along the Chorda Tympani and Trigeminal nerve pathways.

Recognizing that dysgeusia is a documented feature of BMS helps validate that your mouth is not playing tricks on you: the sensory wiring and salivary chemistry are actively reflecting underlying&nbsp;irritation.
