When patients with Burning Mouth Syndrome visit a doctor or dentist, oral dryness is often dismissed based on a quick visual glance: *"Your tongue looks moist, so your salivary glands are fine."*

Yet as our community data demonstrates, **79% of patients deal with chronic dry or sticky saliva**. 

The fundamental problem is that visual inspection is incapable of measuring **salivary flow rate, resting buffering capacity, or biochemical protection**. 

A mouth can look superficially wet while producing severely depressed resting volume or acidic, unbuffered fluid that fails to protect exposed dentin and mucosal nerve&nbsp;beds.

To evaluate whether your salivary defense system is compromised, you need objective data. Here is a breakdown of the official clinical tests performed by oral medicine specialists, alongside practical, validated at-home testing methods you can perform on your&nbsp;own.

## Official Clinical Saliva Tests Performed by Specialists

Oral medicine specialists, hospital stomatology clinics, and rheumatologists use standardized clinical tests to differentiate between **true gland hypofunction** and **neuropathic sensory burning**:

### 1. Unstimulated Whole Sialometry (UWS: The Resting Flow Test)
* **What It Measures:** The baseline saliva produced by your minor salivary glands and submandibular/sublingual glands when you are not eating, chewing, or speaking. This resting film is what coats and protects your teeth and tongue 24 hours a&nbsp;day.
* **The Clinical Protocol:** You sit comfortably with your head tilted slightly forward. For 5 to 15 minutes, you allow saliva to passively pool on the floor of your mouth and expectorate into a graduated collection tube every 60 seconds without swallowing or chewing.
* **Diagnostic Thresholds:**
  * **Normal Resting Flow:** 0.3 to 0.4 mL/min.
  * **Low / Borderline:** 0.1 to 0.25 mL/min.
  * **Objective Hyposalivation (Diagnostic Dry Mouth):** Less than **0.1 mL/min**.

### 2. Stimulated Whole Sialometry (SWS: The Chewing Flow Test)
* **What It Measures:** The maximum functional pumping capacity of your large parotid glands under masticatory stimulation.
* **The Clinical Protocol:** You chew on an unflavored piece of sterile paraffin wax or gum base for 5 minutes, collecting all produced saliva into a calibrated container.
* **Diagnostic Thresholds:**
  * **Normal Stimulated Flow:** 1.0 to 2.0+ mL/min.
  * **Objective Gland Failure:** Less than **0.5 to 0.7 mL/min**.
* **The BMS Pattern:** In many BMS patients, stimulated flow is completely normal (the glands *can* produce saliva when prompted), but **unstimulated resting flow is severely depressed**, leaving tissues vulnerable throughout the day and night.

### 3. Salivary Buffering Capacity and pH Testing (e.g., GC Saliva-Check Buffer)
* **What It Measures:** The concentration of sodium bicarbonate ions in your saliva and its ability to neutralize incoming acids.
* **The Clinical Protocol:** A clinician places drops of stimulated saliva onto colorimetric test pads containing standardized acid reagents.
* **Clinical Significance:** Healthy saliva buffers acid back to neutral (pH 6.8 to 7.4) within 5 to 10 minutes. In patients with low buffer capacity, saliva remains acidic (pH 5.0 to 6.2) for hours, continuously dissolving root cementum and activating **ASIC3** proton receptors.

### 4. Salivary Gland Scintigraphy and Ultrasound
* **What It Measures:** Visual imaging of gland architecture, ductal blockages, and uptake function.
* **Clinical Application:** Often ordered by rheumatologists when ruling out **Sjögren's Syndrome**, autoimmune sialadenitis, or ductal stones (sialolithiasis). A radioactive tracer (Technetium-99m) evaluates how quickly the parotid and submandibular glands absorb and discharge fluid.

### 5. Minor Salivary Gland Lip Biopsy
* **What It Measures:** A small 5 mm incision is made inside the lower lip to harvest 4 to 6 minor salivary glands for histopathological examination.
* **Clinical Purpose:** Considered the gold standard for diagnosing primary Sjögren's Syndrome (measuring lymphocytic focus scores &ge; 1 per 4 mm<sup>2</sup>). If the biopsy is clean, true autoimmune gland destruction is ruled out.

### Step-by-Step Home Sialometry Protocol:
1. **Timing:** Perform the test in the morning, at least 90 minutes after eating, drinking, or brushing.
2. **Tools Needed:** A small sterile medicine cup marked in milliliters (mL) and a timer set to 5&nbsp;minutes.
3. **Execution:** Sit upright, relax your jaw, swallow once to clear your mouth, and set the timer. Do not talk, chew, or swallow. Every 60 seconds, gently spit whatever fluid has pooled into the cup.
4. **Scoring Your Volume:**
   * **1.5 mL to 2.5 mL:** Healthy, robust resting salivary output.
   * **0.6 mL to 1.2 mL:** Mild to moderate reduction in resting lubrication.
   * **Under 0.5 mL (less than 0.1 mL/min):** Severe objective resting hyposalivation.

## How to Interpret Your Results in BMS

When you compare your test results against your pain symptoms, you will land in one of two distinct categories:

### Scenario A: Low Unstimulated Flow + Acidic pH (< 6.5)
* **The Root Cause:** You have **true physical hypofunction**. Your salivary glands are producing insufficient watery volume or lacking bicarbonate buffer.
* **The Consequence:** Without saliva, acids remain unbuffered, bacteria multiply rapidly in dental crevices, and exposed tooth roots suffer continuous chemical demineralization, opening dentinal tubules to direct nerve irritation.
* **Action Steps:** Investigate medication side effects, test for autoimmune markers, train nighttime nasal breathing, and use non-acidic remineralizing pastes.

### Scenario B: Normal or High Flow (> 1.5 mL / 5 min) + Normal pH (6.8 to 7.2)
* **The Root Cause:** You have **neuropathic dysesthesia (Subjective Xerostomia)**.
* **The Consequence:** Your salivary glands are pumping healthy, abundant saliva (just as documented in my clinical exam with Dr. Nathalie Rei), but damaged small-fiber nerve endings in the tongue and palate are firing continuous false error signals to the brain.
* **Action Steps:** Stop chugging gallons of plain water (which washes away protective mucins). Focus on identifying local structural hardware triggers (microleakage under crowns, exposed dentin tubules, galvanic currents) and calming central trigeminal excitability.
