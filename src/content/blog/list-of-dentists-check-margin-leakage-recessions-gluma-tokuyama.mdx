If you have Burning Mouth Syndrome, finding a dentist who does not dismiss you is one of the hardest parts of the journey. Most patients are told: *"Your teeth look fine, there is no infection, go see a neurologist."*

Yet as hundreds of patients in our community have discovered, chronic oral burning is frequently fueled by microscopic physical breaches: **cervical gumline recession exposing dentinal tubules**, and **sub-micron margin leakage beneath crowns or old fillings**.

When a dentist is willing to run simple, non-invasive chairside trials (painting a drop of **Gluma** or **Tokuyama Shield Force Plus** on suspect margins), patients often experience dramatic pain drops within 24 to 48 hours. That single test separates an elusive neurological phantom from an actionable dental leak.

This living directory lists dentists, prosthodontists, and biological or biomimetic practitioners who understand this connection, respect patient symptom tracking, and are willing to evaluate physical micro-leaks.

---

## What We Look for in a BMS-Friendly Dentist

Before booking an appointment, verify that the clinic is willing to perform these four specific evaluations:

1. **Check for Cervical Recession and Abfraction Notches:** Carefully probe exposed root necks along the gumline where enamel is missing and sensitive dentin is exposed to oral fluids.
2. **Audit Crown and Filling Margins with Micro-Explorers:** Run sharp fine-point explorers around 360 degrees of older restorations to feel for cement wash-out, catches, and marginal degradation.
3. **Use High-Magnification or Transillumination (FOTI):** Look beyond standard panoramic X-rays to spot hairline craze lines and micro-fractures that conventional X-rays cannot detect.
4. **Perform In-Office Desensitizer Trials:** Agree to paint **Gluma** (glutaraldehyde and HEMA) or **Tokuyama Shield Force Plus** (monomer resin sealant) over suspect root collars or crown borders as a reversible diagnostic test.

## What to Say When Calling the Clinic

When contacting a new dental office, avoid using complex labels or debating neurological diagnoses on the phone. Receptionists and dentists respond best to simple, mechanical requests:
