When doctors and dentists examine a patient with severe, burning oral pain, they look through magnification loupes, run explorer picks across restorations, and check dental bitewings. When the surface looks intact, they frequently conclude: *"There is no opening here that could cause this level of&nbsp;pain."*

This conclusion is the result of a profound **scale mismatch**.

Dentistry is trained on macroscopic and millimeter-level scales: large cavitated decay, cracked cusps, or deep pocket depths. 

However, the biological conduits that connect the oral cavity directly to pulpal small-fiber nerves: **dentinal tubules**: operate at the **microscopic and nanometer scale**. 

A single receded tooth collar or microleakage margin exposes between **20,000 and 45,000 open tubules per square millimeter**. Each tubule has an outer diameter of roughly **0.8 to 0.9 micrometers (μm)** at the root surface, widening to **2 to 3 μm** as it approaches the pulp.

Understanding how oral inputs interact with these open pipelines explains why normal hygiene practices can worsen the burn, why food particles behave abrasively on the surface, and why microscopic dietary acids and subgingival bacteria trigger instant neuropathic&nbsp;alarms.

## Can Oral Bacteria Actually Enter Dentinal Tubules?

Yes. In oral histopathology, this process is known as **dentinal tubule bacterial invasion**.

* A spherical bacterium like *Streptococcus mutans* or *Enterococcus faecalis* measures between **0.5 and 1.0 micrometer (μm)** in diameter.
* A rod-shaped bacterium like *Lactobacillus* measures approximately **0.5 to 1.0 μm wide**.
* A spiral spirochete like *Treponema denticola* has a cross-sectional width of only **0.15 to 0.25 μm**.

Because dentinal tubules have surface apertures of **0.8 to 0.9 μm** that rapidly widen to **2.0 to 3.0 μm** within the first millimeter, single bacterial cells fit directly into the conduit.

Once subgingival plaque or biofilm establishes itself over an unarmored root collar or a failing crown margin:
1. **Biofilm Anchor:** Bacteria do not remain solely on the exterior tooth surface. Motile and dividing bacteria migrate up to 500 to 1,000 micrometers deep into the open fluid channels.
2. **The Intratubular Acid Pump:** When you eat fermentable carbohydrates or starches, sugars dissolve into the saliva and diffuse into these fluid-filled conduits. Bacteria trapped inside the tubules ferment those sugars into localized **lactic acid**.
3. **Internal Acid Trap:** Because the fluid inside a microscopic pipe is stagnant and shielded from salivary buffering, the internal pH drops rapidly below 5.5. This dissolves peritubular dentin and directly bathes the intratubular nerve endings in high concentrations of free protons.

### 1. The Smear Plug Scouring Effect
Under healthy conditions, exposed dentin is partially protected by a microscopic paste of crushed hydroxyapatite crystals and collagen fibers called the **smear layer** and **smear plugs**. These plugs sit like corks in the neck of each tubule.
* A soft nylon toothbrush bristle has a diameter of **150 to 200 μm**: over 150 times larger than the tubule opening.
* Toothpaste abrasive grit (silica) particles measure **5 to 20 μm**: 10 to 20 times larger than the tubule.

When you scrub a receded root surface with a toothbrush and gritty paste, you are not cleaning inside the tubules. You are **sandpapering the surface**. The abrasive grit shears away the protective smear plugs, unplugging thousands of sealed conduits in a matter of seconds.

### 2. The Hydrodynamic Piston Effect
Once the smear plugs are scoured off:
* Chewing firm foods (nuts, crusts, meat fibers) creates localized mechanical pressure gradients across the tooth collar.
* In accordance with Brännström's **Hydrodynamic Theory of Dentin Sensitivity**, external pressure or thermal shifts rapidly displace fluid inside the tubule.
* Because fluid is incompressible, this movement creates a rapid piston stroke: dragging, pulling, and deforming the unmyelinated intratubular nerve endings and odontoblast processes at the pulp junction.

* A free hydronium ion / proton (H+) has an effective ionic radius of less than **0.001 micrometers** (sub-nanometer: roughly 0.15 nm).
* Compared to an 800-nanometer tubule entrance, an acid proton is **over 5,000,000 times smaller** than the pipe opening.

When you sip black coffee (pH 4.8 to 5.2), wine (pH 3.2 to 3.8), citrus water (pH 2.5 to 3.0), or vinegar-based dressings, there is zero physical barrier preventing those protons from traveling instantly down the dentinal fluid column.

Within seconds:
1. **Chemical Dissolution:** The acid dissolves the mineral walls of the tubule, preventing natural saliva remineralization.
2. **Receptor Docking:** Protons bind directly to the external vestibules of **ASIC1** and **ASIC3** channels located on the unmyelinated nerve endings.
3. **Threshold Ignition:** The massive influx of positive ions charges the terminal membrane past its -55 mV firing threshold, igniting high-frequency action potentials that flood into the trigeminal system.

## The First-Principles Takeaway for BMS Management

Understanding the nanoscale gate reframes how we address Burning Mouth Syndrome:

1. **Stop Scrubbing the Root Face:** Using stiff brushes, abrasive whitening pastes (RDA > 70), or aggressive interdental scrubbing scours away the only natural mineral plugs standing between oral acids and your pulpal nerves.
2. **Recognize the True Biological Breach:** A tooth does not need a large macroscopic cavity to trigger a full-mouth neuropathic alarm. 20,000 to 45,000 open sub-micron tubules are more than enough to saturate the sensory cable.
3. **Shield the Conduits:** Recovery requires mechanical hardware sealing (composite bonding, varnishes, sealants, or glutaraldehyde desensitizers) to physically cap the open tubules, coupled with strict elimination of the dietary acids that keep the internal proton sensors locked open.
